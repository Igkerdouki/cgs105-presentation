06/15

Rubin's Warning

"Intersex Exceptionalism"

Up to this point, Rubin has shown how intersex can challenge assumptions in medicine and feminist theory. But here Rubin warns about a different problem.

This happens when we treat intersex bodies as extraordinary cases that reveal some spectacular truth about gender—as though everyone else's body is straightforward and the system only becomes complicated when an intersex person appears.

"Bodies which are suspect are not what have to be explained. Rather, the requirement that they explain themselves should itself be investigated."

— David Valentine & Riki Anne Wilchins, quoted in Rubin

Better approach

Use intersex as a starting point for thinking about how all bodies get categorized—not as a special exception that exists just to prove a theory.

Why is this one body
asked to explain everything?

07/15

The Opening Anecdote

Does Studying Intersex
Threaten "Women"?

Rubin opens with a story from a job interview. A senior faculty member hears someone mention intersex and transgender studies and responds with alarm. This captures a fear: if feminist studies questions the category "woman," does that category disappear?

Rubin's Point

Studying intersex does not mean we stop talking about women. It means thinking more carefully about how that category is defined and where its boundaries come from.

Intersex doesn't make "women" unusable—it exposes uncertainties in how bodies are understood and classified.

Questioning ≠ Eliminating

Small Group Activity

2–3 Minutes: Think individually or discuss in chat

Think of a classification system in your field or daily life:

  • What happens to cases that don't fit?
  • Are cases that don't fit treated as problems to fix—or signals that the system may need revision?
  • Who decides?

Examples: forms/databases, medical categories, school systems, algorithms, sports, workplace policies…

Then we'll hear 2–3 examples.
